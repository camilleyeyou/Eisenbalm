01 How it started

It started with one dog who could still think, eat and wag — but couldn’t walk.

The dog was paralyzed. Everything else about it was fine: sharp, hungry, glad to see people. The usual answer for a dog like that is to put it down. The founders saw it differently. The dog was fine. The legs weren’t. That’s a logistics problem.

The fix was a two-wheeled cart with a harness fitted to that particular dog. It worked. And it showed what the real job would be: not inventing a device, but getting the right one, fitting it properly, and checking back after.

The early days were hard in unglamorous ways. Finding parts. Getting the fit right on dogs of every shape. Following up with owners once the dog went home.

They chose this work on purpose because the result is easy to see. A dog either walks again or it doesn’t. There’s no fuzzy “impact” to explain.

“The dog was fine. The legs weren’t. That’s a logistics problem.”

When a dog loses its back legs, most owners are scared and short on time. If no one tells them a cart is an option, or they can’t afford one, euthanasia becomes the default. Not because the dog is suffering, but because there’s no plan B on the table.

Buying a cart online isn’t the whole answer either. A cart that doesn’t fit can rub, tip or strain the dog. It needs measuring, adjusting and checking as the dog gets used to it.

That gap — between “there’s a device for this” and “this dog is walking” — is exactly where this nonprofit works.

03 The founder

The founder would rather you remember the dogs.

You won’t find the founder’s name in the press. They avoid the spotlight and think about the work the way an engineer would: parts, sizes, fit, follow-up.

Their main design goal was one cart that adjusts across breeds, so a dachshund and a shepherd don’t each need a custom build.

Later they added something most cart makers skip: training for owners. How to put the harness on. Which ground is safe. The signs that a cart needs adjusting before it causes a sore.

04 One dog’s story

No back legs on arrival. A few fittings later, walking.

The dog came in with no function in its back legs. The first fitting was a start, not a finish. It took several rounds of measuring and adjusting before the cart sat right.

Once it did, the dog was getting around on its own. And the rest followed: it ate better, slept better and went back to being social with people and other dogs.

The lesson the team takes from cases like this: the cart is the easy part. The careful fitting, and coming back to check, is what actually gets a dog moving.

“The cart is the easy part. The fitting is the work.”
